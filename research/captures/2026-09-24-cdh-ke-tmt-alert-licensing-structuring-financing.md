# FULL SOURCE TEXT (captured)

data centre sector has grown quickly on the back of submarine cable landings and rising cloud and artificial intelligence driven demand, even as its regulatory treatment continues to evolve. The most recent regulatory developments include the Revised Telecommunications Market Structure (Revised Structure) gazetted on 6 March 2026 and the Communications Authority of Kenya’s (Authority) public notice issued on 8 September 2026 opening a 30-day comment window on its proposal to set up a licensing framework for data centres that would introduce a standalone data centre licence category, rather than continuing to regulate them under Network Facilities Provider – Tier 2. 23 Sep 2026 6 min read Technology & Communications Alert Article Authors Njeri Wagacha Partner, East Africa Lead: Industrials, Manufacturing and Trade Sector Corporate & Commercial Law Brian Macharia Associate Corporate & Commercial Law Related sectors Technology & Communications Related services Corporate & Commercial Law At a glance On 8 September 2026, the Communications Authority of Kenya (Authority) published a public notice opening a 30-day comment window on its proposal to set up a licensing framework for data centres that would introduce a standalone data centre licence category, rather than continuing to regulate them under Network Facilities Provider – Tier 2. Under the Authority’s current Revised Telecommunications Market Structure gazetted on 6 March 2026, data centres are regulated under the Network Facilities Provider – Tier 2 category, while holders of a Network Facilities Provider – Tier 1 licence may rely on that existing infrastructure licence to operate a commercial data centre. Recent Kenyan projects show two principal structuring routes: a pan-African platform model funded at group level, and a standalone Kenyan project company financed on a single-asset basis. Land tenure, power strategy and financing structure are the main early decisions, because each affects approvals, tax treatment, security packages, construction timing and overall project cost. This alert draws on recently completed Kenyan projects, namely Airtel Africa’s Nxtra facility at Tatu City, iXAfrica’s Nairobi One campus, and Cassava Technologies’ Africa Data Centres, to set out some of the regulatory approvals a project of this kind will require and how comparable projects are actually being structured commercially. The result is a market in which the threshold question is increasingly commercial rather than regulatory: how to house the project, secure land and power, fund construction and allocate risk between the operating company, lenders, customers and any strategic partners. Why this matters for telecommunications businesses Kenya’s data centre sector is maturing quickly, with well-financed competition emerging even as parts of the regulatory picture remain in motion. Telecommunications businesses are increasingly evaluating data centres as infrastructure plays rather than adjacent technology projects, and the key decisions are structural: whether to fund a pan-African platform or a standalone Kenyan project company; whether to acquire land directly or locate within a special economic zone; and whether to build around conventional construction, modular expansion or captive power arrangements. Those choices affect licensing, tax, security, lender control rights and timing, and should be settled, factoring in the potential upcoming regulatory developments, before term sheets are exchanged. Key regulatory approvals Telecommunications licensing The Authority’s power to licence telecommunications activity derives from section 24(1) of the Kenya Information and Communications Act, Chapter 411A of the Laws of Kenya, which prohibits operating a telecommunications system or providing telecommunications services without a valid licence. The Unified Licensing Framework (ULF) itself dates back to 2008, adopted as a technology- and service-neutral regime so that a single licence category can cover any underlying technology. Under the Authority’s Revised Structure, the ULF Annex III licence table names Network Facilities Provider – Tier 2 as the category under which data centres are licensed. Network Facilities Provider – Tier 1 is the separate, nationwide network infrastructure licence, and its holders are simply permitted to establish commercial data centres without an additional licence, rather than being licensed for data centres as such. The Authority’s proposed replacement for this treatment would introduce a standalone data centre licence category for co-location operators, on the basis that they provide hosting, power, cooling and storage infrastructure rather than telecommunications services as such. Entities that already hold a Network Facilities Provider or Application Service Provider licence would be exempt from needing the new licence. The proposed licence would have a 15-year term, with an application fee of KES 5,000, an initial fee of KES 100,000, and an annual operating fee of the higher of KES 80,000 or 0.4% of annual gross turnover. The proposal remains open for public comment for 30 days from publication and is not yet in force; the Authority’s own implementation roadmap targets finalisation in the 2026/27 financial year, and rollout in the 2027/28 financial year. Energy licensing An energy licence from the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) is required for any on-site power generation above the exempt threshold, and a separate approval is needed if that power is transmitted to other users rather than consumed entirely on-site. County and environmental approvals Change of user (where applicable), approval of architectural and structural plans and a certificate of occupation from the relevant county government, together with an environmental impact assessment licence from the National Environment Management Authority given the scale, power and water demands, and any diesel back-up generation, of a facility of this kind. Data protection and localisation A facility operator will typically need to register with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner as a data controller and/or processor, and complete a data protection impact assessment where personal data will be processed at scale. Where that data relates to defined strategic state purposes such as civil registration, elections administration or public finance systems, the Data Protection (General) Regulations, 2021 go further and require at least one serving copy in a Kenya-based data centre, a localisation requirement that may work in an operator’s favour commercially if its facility is positioned to host these government workloads. Typical deal structures: How the market actually splits Project vehicle Kenyan precedent does not point to one model. Airtel’s Nxtra and Cassava’s Africa Data Centres are both delivered through pan-African group subsidiaries, with investors funding the parent data-centre business across several countries at once rather than lending against any single facility’s own contracts and land. iXAfrica, by contrast, is a standalone Kenyan project company financed and secured on a single-asset basis. This choice between the two models drives every downstream question of security, tax and lender approval rights, and should be made deliberately early in any transaction. Land tenure Comparable projects have used three different approaches: direct acquisition (iXAfrica’s Mombasa Road site and its agreement to purchase 11 acres in Tilisi for a second campus), siting within an existing special economic zone (SEZ) (Airtel’s Nxtra, at Tatu City SEZ), and phased expansion on an already-controlled adjacent site (Africa Data Centres’ Nairobi facility). Land and title diligence carries elevated weight in these deals given that the assets are immovable, capital intensive and often built on long leaseholds, consistent with the general 99-year leasehold ceiling that applies to non-citizen-owned entities under Article 65 of the Constitution and the Land Act, Chapter 280 of the Laws of Kenya. Construction and power Where a facility generates its own power on-site, as is the case at Nxtra, which draws grid power from a dedicated substation topped up with on-site solar. The solar portion triggers the EPRA energy licence noted above, together with its own construction contract, run alongside the main building works contract. Africa Data Centres instead builds using prefabricated, factory-assembled modules rather than building everything on-site from scratch, a genuine alternative worth costing against a conventional build. Financing Nairobi deals typically combine debt secured against a facility’s long-term customer contracts with a more flexible layer of equity or subordinated debt to fund capacity that is not yet leased out; iXAfrica’s financing, built on earlier equity from Helios and debt from Rand Merchant Bank, is the clearest local example. Development finance institutions are also a recurring funding source (the US International Development Finance Corporation has lent up to USD 300 million to Africa Data Centres across several countries). Because building costs are paid in US dollars while revenue is a mix of dollars and shillings, lenders typically require funds to be collected offshore or protected against currency swings. A single large, long-term customer contract matches a long-term loan well; shorter, smaller customer contracts do not. That mismatch is why lenders in this market increasingly split financing into several layers rather than issuing one loan. Other matters worth noting Competition clearance Established operators with significant market share should note that any joint venture, acquisition or material partnership structure for a data centre project may need to be screened early for Competition Authority of Kenya notification thresholds, particularly given the fragmenting enterprise-hosting market: Airtel’s Nxtra facility has been widely described in Kenyan media as a challenge to Safaricom’s position, while iXAfrica already hosts Safaricom’s own enterprise clients. Cybersecurity Compliance obligations under the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act and the Authority’s sector cybersecurity guidelines should be built into the facility’s security and incident-response documentation from the outset. Lessons for telecommunications businesses Taken together, these projects show there’s no single “correct” way to structure a Kenyan data centre – the financing model, land strategy and construction method all carry distinct licensing, financing and tax consequences that should be resolved before term sheets are exchanged. Companies should seek legal advice to discuss these issues against a specific project’s parameters and prepare a tailored structuring and approvals roadmap.
