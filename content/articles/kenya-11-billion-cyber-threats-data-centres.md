In October 2026 the Communications Authority of Kenya announced that the country had detected 11.1 billion cyber-threat events during the 2025/26 financial year, up 29.0 percent on the year before, alongside 83.1 million advisories issued to affected ICT users, up 60.8 percent. Most coverage will stop at the number, add the word alarming, and move on. The number deserves a different reading, because detection at that scale is not a software story. It is an infrastructure story, and it is one of the cleanest demand signals for Kenyan data centres published all year.

> **What the 11.1 billion means.** The figure counts detected cyber-threat events reported through Kenya's national cyber-response infrastructure. It does not mean that 11.1 billion individual systems were breached, and it does not mean every event was a successful attack. That distinction matters for anyone making infrastructure decisions on the back of this number, so it is kept unavoidable throughout this article.

## What the number is, and what it is not

The bulletins behind the announcement are precise about what is being counted, and the precision is what makes the figure useful rather than frightening. Read it as a national systems-health reading: hostile traffic seen and logged by monitoring that exists, is staffed and publishes its methods. Not as a body count of victims, and not as a loss figure either.

| The figure is | The figure is not |
| --- | --- |
| Detected events reported through national monitoring | Confirmed breaches |
| A national monitoring output | 11.1 billion separate victims |
| A volume signal | A measure of successful attacks |
| Based on reported detection | A direct measure of financial loss |

None of that diminishes the signal; it frames it. What grew 29.0 percent is what the country's monitoring can see, and building the capacity to see more is an infrastructure decision.

The quarter-by-quarter build of the total is public, and it is what separates this article from a press release. The National KE-CIRT/CC, the country's 24/7 computer incident response team operating within the Authority, publishes quarterly cyber security reports, and the four quarters of FY 2025/26 add up almost exactly to the headline: 842,320,667 detected events in July to September 2025, 4,559,229,985 in October to December, 3,367,113,840 in January to March 2026, and 2,355,938,192 in April to June. That sums to 11,124,602,684, which rounds to the 11.1 billion in the announcement. The gap between the quietest quarter and the loudest is more than five times, with October to December spiking 441.27 percent before the year cooled off. Swings of that size are normal in national telemetry; the honest reading is the annual total and its trend, not any single quarter.

## What a billion detections actually requires

A detected threat event is the output of a chain that has to exist somewhere physically. Telemetry has to be collected on networks, exchanged between operators, correlated against known patterns, stored, and reviewed by analysts on shift. The National KE-CIRT/CC sits at the national end of that chain, and since the Computer Misuse and Cybercrime (Critical Information Infrastructure and Cybercrime Management) Regulations 2024 came into force, the Authority's mandate explicitly includes establishing and operating a Cyber Security Operations Centre (CSOC) for the ICT and telecommunications sector. The 83.1 million advisories, 20,748,489 of them in the final quarter alone, are the human-readable output of all that machinery: every advisory assumed an ICT user somewhere had the connectivity to receive it and the infrastructure to act on it.

The rest of the chain looks like this: users generate traffic, carrier networks carry it, [the Kenya Internet Exchange Point](/articles/kixp-internet-exchange-point-kenya) keeps local traffic local, data centres and cloud platforms host the services being attacked, facility teams watch their own halls, and the national team correlates what everyone sees.

```text
Traffic
  ↓
Carrier networks
  ↓
IXPs and data centres
  ↓
Telemetry collection
  ↓
National CSOC / KE-CIRT
  ↓
Advisories and response
```

Each rung of that ladder is a physical system somebody has to build, power, connect and staff, which is why the chain reads as an infrastructure bill of materials rather than a diagram in a strategy deck.

Kenya has a relatively mature version of that chain for the region, combining national response capability, an established IXP ecosystem and a growing data-centre market. The [ISOC Pulse IXP Tracker](https://pulse.internetsociety.org/en/ixp-tracker/country/KE/) reports, on its October 2026 PeeringDB sync, that 89 percent of Kenya's 225 active networks are either IXP members themselves or customers of IXP members, and PeeringDB's record for the Nairobi exchange lists 144 member networks with about 2,985 Gbps of connected port capacity. The exchange fabric is therefore a genuine national vantage point rather than a boutique facility. Detection capacity and peering capacity are, in practice, the same investment.

## Where the detection infrastructure sits

The DC254 map answers the where question directly. Nairobi holds 19 of Kenya's 27 tracked facilities, 13 of them operational, and that cluster is where the halls, NOCs and interconnection fabric concentrate. Mombasa is the other half of the story: all four of its tracked facilities are operational, and it is the coast where 7 in-service submarine cable systems come ashore (of 10 tracked, with Africa-1 landed and still awaiting RFS, plus Daraja announced and LuLu planned). The market's published live IT load is 10.5 MW, with 8 of the 20 operational facilities publishing figures, live designed capacity stands at 42.9 MW, and the announced pipeline adds 230 MW more. The build-out creates more of the physical capacity on which detection, storage, analysis and response can run, but only where security workloads, connectivity and skilled teams are actually provisioned. That is the defensible version of the claim, and it is also the commercially interesting one: the facilities that provision for it are the ones that win the regulated tenants.

The bulletins make the security relevance of that geography explicit. In the final quarter of FY 2025/26 the national team counted 17,406,495 web application attack attempts, a 43.68 percent increase on the quarter before, targeted at the critical information infrastructure sector, with government systems and internet service providers the primary targets. Kenya's [documented breach history](/articles/kenya-data-breach-timeline) says the same thing from the incident side: the 2023 eCitizen DDoS degraded the gateway to more than 5,000 public services (BBC, 28 July 2023), and in July 2026 the president's own website was defaced with a Bitcoin ransom note (The Record, 21 July 2026). The attack types are the ones catalogued in [our attack scenarios guide](/articles/data-centre-attack-scenarios-kenya); what is growing is the volume behind them, and the [critical information infrastructure rules](/articles/kenya-ict-policy-framework-data-centres) now formally assume operators are wired into the national detection effort.

## Why the number is a demand signal

Every one of those 11.1 billion events consumed something real: bandwidth on a carrier network, packets across an exchange switch, log storage on a rack, minutes of an analyst's shift. Every one of the 83.1 million advisories travelled over Kenyan networks to someone who then had to patch, reconfigure or at least read. Some of that monitoring can be delivered remotely, but response latency, regulatory accountability, data governance and access to critical infrastructure make local and regionally accountable capability increasingly important, and the 2024 regulations assume the systems watching Kenya's infrastructure are reachable and answerable. Facilities that can demonstrate layered controls, from the network edge down to [the physical layer of data centre security](/articles/data-centre-physical-security-kenya), are the ones that win the regulated workloads, the banks and government tenants, whose telemetry the CSOC exists to watch in the first place.

![An uninterruptible power supply and switchgear room in a data centre](/images/dc-ups-power-room.webp)

That is why this statistic belongs in the infrastructure conversation rather than the fear conversation. Kenya's digital economy is adding load from AI, cloud computing, financial services and digital public platforms at the same time as the threat volume grows, and both curves land on the same racks. The 230 MW pipeline is the country building the capacity to absorb both curves at once. Read the number as a national systems-health reading: hostile traffic is rising, and so is the country's ability to see it, count it and answer it.

## What this means for operators

For the people running Kenyan facilities, the FY 2025/26 numbers translate into a working checklist rather than an abstraction. Detection at national scale assumes somebody retained the logs, so log retention and storage capacity stop being housekeeping and become part of the country's visibility. The bulletins' own attribution of the volume to unpatched systems makes patching discipline and security monitoring coverage the first-order controls, and DDoS protection matters on the evidence too, with 819,325 denial-of-service attempts counted in the final quarter alone. Network segmentation is what keeps a compromise in one tenant zone from becoming a compromise of the hall.

The physical and the human controls belong on the same list. Physical access controls are the layer most often underestimated, because tailgating and unlogged access defeat the best firewalls; [the attack types that hit Kenyan data infrastructure](/articles/data-centre-security-threats-kenya) mostly arrive over the network, but the incidents that do lasting damage tend to combine both. Incident-response staffing, recovery testing and customer notification processes decide whether an incident is an event or a headline, and the expectations are set out in [our disaster recovery and business continuity guide](/articles/disaster-recovery-business-continuity-kenya). For facilities designated as critical information infrastructure, incident reporting to KE-CIRT/CC and compliance with the national security standards are already obligations under the 2024 regulations, not aspirations.

None of this requires exotic spending. It requires the boring, auditable kind: documented access control, tested recovery, evidence of monitoring coverage and a named person who answers when the national team calls. Operators that already run layered defences treat the regulations as documentation work; those that have not should start at [the physical layer of data centre security](/articles/data-centre-physical-security-kenya) and work upward.

Cybersecurity is not separate from digital infrastructure. It is one of the reasons digital infrastructure exists. The 11.1 billion is worth knowing, but the more useful question sits behind it: what does a country need to have built, powered, connected and staffed to see that number at all? Kenya's current answer, visible on [the DC254 map](/infrastructure/map), is 27 tracked facilities, 20 operational, an exchange point that touches nearly every network in the country, and a pipeline that keeps growing. The threat data and the construction data are the same story told twice.
