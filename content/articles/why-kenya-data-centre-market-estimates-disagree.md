Ask three research firms for the size of Kenya's data centre market and you will get three incompatible answers. Xalam Analytics, in its Kenya briefing commissioned through the EU's Digital Investment Facility, puts annual commercial colocation revenue at roughly **US$30 million**. Ken Research's July 2026 syndicated report sizes the market at **US$266 million in 2025**, heading to US$805 million by 2031. Mordor Intelligence lands at **about US$606 million in 2025**. These are not small disagreements about rounding — the spread between the narrowest and the broadest figure is a factor of twenty, and every one of these numbers gets cited, in decks and articles and investment memos, as if it meant the same thing as the others.

We pulled apart what each estimate actually measures. The disagreement is real, it is systematic, and once you see the mechanics you can read any market figure in this industry — Kenya's or anyone's — in about thirty seconds.

![Server racks representing colocation capacity](/images/dc-servers-racks.webp)

## Reason one: scope — five industries wearing one name

"Data centre market" is not one market. It bundles at least five distinct revenue pools, and every research firm draws the circle in a different place:

- **Commercial colocation** — the rent and power that operators earn from selling racks, suites and cross-connects. This is the narrowest, hardest definition, and it is what Xalam's ~US$30 million measures: the actual revenue of facilities you can buy space in, in Nairobi and Mombasa, today.
- **Enterprise and self-built facilities** — banks, telcos and the government running their own data rooms. There is real capital and operating spend here, but almost none of it shows up as market revenue, and almost all of it is invisible outside the owning organisation.
- **Construction and equipment** — the build cost of new campuses. A single 20 MW campus can represent US$100–200 million of capex; including construction inflates any "market size" instantly and makes it swing violently with the project cycle.
- **Cloud and managed services** — revenue from cloud platforms, hosting and managed infrastructure delivered from Kenya. This is a services market that happens to sit in data centres, not a data centre market; including it multiplies the headline.
- **Connectivity** — transit, IP bandwidth and interconnection revenue associated with data centres, sometimes folded in, usually not.

Ken Research's US$266 million and Mordor's US$606 million are total-market figures that include several of these pools. Xalam's US$30 million deliberately includes exactly one. None of the three is wrong; each answers a different question. The failure happens at citation time, when a total-market figure gets quoted in a sentence about operator revenue.

## Reason two: the numbers move — and sources rarely date-stamp them

Market figures travel without their timestamps. Ken Research's own Kenya numbers are the cleanest local example: its 2022 report cycle described the market as roughly US$50 million in 2022, forecast to about US$130 million by 2027. Its July 2026 report puts 2025 at US$266 million — roughly double the trajectory the same publisher described four years earlier, largely because the scope and the market both moved. Neither figure is false; quoting the old one as current is.

The currency layer makes this worse. Kenya's shilling moved sharply against the dollar across this period, and a market measured in shillings but reported in USD swings with the exchange rate even when nothing physical changes. FX movements alone can shift a USD-denominated market size by double-digit percentages between report vintages — enough to explain a chunk of the spread on their own.

Then there is simple staleness. In a market growing at a projected ~30% a year, a figure that was accurate in 2023 is materially wrong in 2026. Any number you are handed without a vintage attached should be treated as decoration.

## Reason three: method — three roads to three answers

Even with identical scope and dates, the estimation method changes the answer:

- **Supply-side build-up** starts from facilities: count the buildings, multiply by capacity and achieved pricing. Xalam's facility-level work is this school, which is why its figures are smaller and harder — it caps at what physically exists and what it can realistically earn. Its Kenya briefing counts roughly 15–20 live facilities and ~13 MW of live IT load, cross-referenced against operator disclosures.
- **Demand-side survey** asks buyers what they spend and scales up. This captures spend the supply side misses (enterprise own-build, services) but inherits survey noise, sample framing and respondent optimism.
- **Top-down modelling** takes a global or regional number, applies a country share based on GDP, connectivity or investment flows, and arrives at the market "implied". This is the fastest way to a big number and the easiest way to a wrong one, because it smooths over the fact that data centre investment is lumpy, project-driven and nothing like proportional to GDP.

Ken Research's published methodology describes triangulating supply-side, operational and demand-side views (its Kenya base case is presented as a weighted result of three estimates with a stated range). That is more rigour than most press releases bother with — but template language recurs across its reports, and no named survey instrument or expert network is published, so the triangulation is more asserted than demonstrated. We say that as an observation about verification, not an accusation: **if a methodology cannot be checked, it cannot be cited with confidence**, which is the same standard we hold our own [directory data](/methodology) to.

## Facility counts: the same disease, smaller numbers

The definitional chaos repeats at the facility level. The PeeringDB register lists 14 Kenya records — but that includes a cable landing station, and one apparent duplicate registration. Round-ups across the web claim 20-plus "data centres in Kenya", typically by counting every server room a telco ever mentioned. Our own verified count is 24 entries — but 14 of those match the register, the rest are operator-owned, government or announced projects, and each is labelled so you can separate operating capacity from ambition.

We caught this firsthand. Our directory previously carried two facilities that failed verification when we checked operator pages and registers in September 2026 — one operator's own website does not list Kenya among its markets at all. They are gone, and the correction is logged publicly. Facility counts are the floor of all market sizing; if the count is contaminated, every figure built on it inherits the contamination.

## A field guide: reading any Kenya market number in thirty seconds

When someone quotes you a figure, walk this checklist:

1. **What revenue pool?** Colocation only, or colocation plus enterprise plus construction plus cloud? If the sentence says "market size" but the number is US$30M-scale, it is colo revenue; if it is hundreds of millions, it is a total-market figure. Both are fine — do not mix them.
2. **What year, and what currency vintage?** A 2023 figure in a 30%-growth market is off by a third. Ask when the underlying data was collected, not when the press release was issued.
3. **Who published it, and can you see the method?** A named methodology you can inspect beats a bigger number you cannot. Press releases and reseller listings are marketing; report pages with visible tables and stated triangulation are closer to evidence.
4. **Does it survive contact with the facilities?** Any Kenya estimate should be roughly consistent with what physically exists: ~13 MW of live IT load, around 60% occupancy, a handful of genuinely carrier-neutral buildings. A figure implying ten times the operating base is measuring something else — usually the future, labelled as the present.
5. **Is the facility count clean?** Counts above the mid-twenties for Kenya, unqualified, have almost always absorbed unverified entries.

## Why this matters more in Nairobi than in Frankfurt

In mature markets, scope disagreements produce small spreads because the colo layer is large relative to everything else. In frontier markets like Kenya, the colo layer is tiny and the announced pipeline is enormous — so the spread between definitions is maximised, and so is the temptation to cite the flattering number. The US$30 million figure is unglamorous and honest: Kenya's commercial colocation industry currently earns about what a single mid-sized European facility does. The US$606 million figure describes a market that will not exist until the pipeline — the 18 MW iXAfrica second building, the ADC expansion, the Microsoft–G42 project if it proceeds — actually opens. Both are useful. Only one of them is present tense.
