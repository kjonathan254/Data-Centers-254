Ethiopia inaugurated the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on 9 September 2025, and within a year it announced the decision that matters most to this platform's readers: electricity exports to Kenya will double from 200 MW to 400 MW starting December 2026 (Ethiopian Electric Power announcements, 16-17 September 2026). The announcement landed in the same season as two other data points worth holding together: Ethiopia reported 118.1 million US dollars in export earnings from electricity sales to Kenya, Djibouti, Sudan and Tanzania in the 2024/25 financial year (EEP, reported June 2026), and regional utilities committed to a 1.6 billion US dollar investment push to deepen East African power trade (16 September 2026).

For most coverage, this is an energy story or a diplomacy story. For anyone building, financing or buying data centre capacity in Nairobi, it is a supply story: Kenya now hosts 27 verified data centre facilities with 38.5 MW of live designed capacity and a 230 MW announced pipeline (DC254 directory, verified 2026), and every megawatt of that build-out needs firm, priced, contractable electricity. A 2,000 MW HVDC highway arriving from a hydro-rich neighbour changes the supply picture in ways that are easy to overstate and easy to miss. This article separates the two.

## The line: what the Eastern Electricity Highway actually is

The Ethiopia-Kenya interconnector is a 500 kV bipolar high-voltage direct current transmission line running roughly 1,069 kilometres from Wolayta Sodo in southern Ethiopia to Suswa in Kenya's Rift Valley, crossing the border at Moyale (KETRACO, April 2025; African Development Bank project record). It is rated to carry 2,000 MW, which makes it the largest power transfer path in East Africa by a wide margin. The World Bank and the African Development Bank financed the programme, and both institutions describe it as the anchor of the wider Eastern Electricity Highway Project, a deliberate attempt to build a regional electricity market rather than a set of isolated national grids.

![Map of the 500 kV transmission line route from southern Ethiopia through Moyale to Gilgil and Naivasha in Kenya](/images/ethiopia-kenya-500kv-route-map.webp)

Commercial flows began in 2022, under a 25-year power supply arrangement between Ethiopian Electric Power and Kenya Power. The initial price was reported at about 6.5 US cents per kWh, and Kenya quickly became the biggest foreign buyer of Ethiopian power (reporting, February 2025). The choice of direct current technology is not incidental: over a distance like this, HVDC loses less energy, needs a narrower corridor, and crucially allows two asynchronously operated grids to trade power without synchronising their whole systems. The trade-off is converter infrastructure at both ends, which is where the cost and the operational complexity concentrate.

## Suswa: where the highway lands in Kenya

![Capacitor stacks and valve equipment at the Suswa HVDC converter station in Kenya](/images/suswa-hvdc-converter-station.webp)

Every imported megawatt arrives at Suswa as direct current and is converted back to alternating current before it enters Kenya's transmission network. That makes the Suswa converter station the single most critical node on the entire corridor: if conversion capacity trips, the imports stop, regardless of how much energy Ethiopia has available. KETRACO's maintenance updates through 2025 underline the point that this is active, specialised infrastructure, not passive wire. From Suswa, power flows into the Kenyan grid around the Rift Valley and toward the Nairobi metropolitan load centre, where it mixes with geothermal from Olkaria, hydro from the Tana and Seven Forks complexes, wind from Turkana, and increasingly solar.

The practical takeaway for power buyers is that imports strengthen the system's energy position without changing its delivery physics. The electrons still travel the same national grid as everything else, with the same transmission constraints, switching events and outage risks described in our reliability analysis. An interconnector diversifies where energy comes from; it does not shorten the path to the rack.

## GERD: the machine behind the exports

![The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile photographed at dusk](/images/gerd-dam-dusk.webp)

Ethiopia's ability to sell 200 MW, and soon 400 MW, to Kenya rests on the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile. Construction began in 2011, and the plant was officially inaugurated on 9 September 2025 with an installed capacity of 5,150 MW across 13 generating units in two powerhouses, producing on average about 15,700 GWh per year (inauguration reporting, September 2025). For scale, that annual energy figure is comparable to a meaningful fraction of Kenya's entire national consumption, which is why the dam changes regional arithmetic rather than just Ethiopian arithmetic.

![Water pouring down the GERD spillway with Ethiopian flags on the dam face and transmission lines leaving the site](/images/gerd-dam-spillway.webp)

The commercial results are already visible. Ethiopia earned 118.1 million US dollars from electricity exports to Kenya, Djibouti, Sudan and Tanzania in the 2024/25 financial year (EEP, reported June 2026), with Kenya the largest single buyer. Ethiopia has framed these revenues as a strategic earner, and the September 2026 announcement that exports to Kenya will double from December 2026 is the concrete expression of that strategy. The energy is firm hydro stored behind the continent's largest dam, but it is still hydro, which means the export commitment is exposed to hydrology in a way geothermal is not, a risk Kenya knows how to price from its own drought years.

![A crane lowering a turbine generator rotor into place inside the GERD powerhouse](/images/gerd-generator-rotor-installation.webp)

The construction story is worth respecting on its own terms. Installing thirteen generating units, commissioning them in stages through 2022 to 2025, and building the transmission spine to move the power 1,069 km south was executed largely with domestic financing, in a country where only about half the population has reliable access to electricity. The paradox is deliberate: export revenue from surplus generation funds the expansion of domestic access, which is the same argument Kenya makes for its own regional hub ambitions.

![Engineers operating switchgear inside the GERD powerhouse beneath a large Ethiopian flag](/images/gerd-powerhouse-ethiopian-flag.webp)

## What the power costs, and why buyers care

The original 2022 arrangement priced Ethiopian hydropower at about 6.5 US cents per kWh. Mid-2026 reporting on the renewed supply arrangement indicated the reviewed price roughly doubles that figure, and trade coverage in September 2026 framed it as Kenya Power paying double for the same imports (September 2026). Two context points keep that in proportion. First, even the reviewed price remains below the cost of Kenyan thermal peaking generation, which can run above 20 US cents per kWh, and imports therefore still function as a hedge that displaces the most expensive marginal generation. Second, the price is denominated in foreign currency, so shilling depreciation passes through to the landed cost, a mechanic covered in our tariff analysis.

For a data centre operator, the relevant question is never the average national tariff but the contracted stack behind their own meter. Imports at any price add energy to the bulk system, which relieves system-level stress, supports the case against emergency thermal dispatch, and improves the odds that the marginal megawatt in a constrained hour is renewable rather than diesel. None of that changes a facility's contracted capacity charge, its standby obligations, or the engineering it must fund behind the meter.

## What a data centre connection actually requires

![Schematic of a grid connection from a high-voltage substation through switchgear and transformers to a data centre](/images/diagram-grid-connection-anatomy.webp)

It is tempting to read a headline like "2,000 MW arriving from Ethiopia" as if a data centre could tap it directly. The reality is the chain pictured above: a high-voltage substation and transformer, medium-voltage switchgear, distribution transformation, and often reactive compensation or battery storage before power reaches a facility. Every one of those elements carries its own lead time, and in a market building as quickly as Nairobi, the connection queue matters as much as the generation queue. A megawatt that exists on a transmission line but has no substation capacity behind it is, from a buyer's chair, not yet real.

![Schematic of a data centre microgrid combining grid supply, on-site generation and battery storage](/images/diagram-datacentre-power-microgrid.webp)

That is why Kenyan facilities continue to engineer the full stack behind the meter regardless of what the grid delivers: UPS bridges for the seconds, on-site generation for the hours, and increasingly storage and even dedicated generation for the days, as the Amaco HERCULES barge plan in Mombasa takes to its logical extreme. Imports enrich the options in the bulk system; the facility-level engineering, the part that turns electricity into contracted uptime, is unchanged. Buyers evaluating Nairobi capacity should read the import headlines as improving the odds on the grid's average day, and keep pricing the building for its worst one.

## The demand side: what 400 MW would meet in Nairobi

![Server racks and an Atlancis-branded cabinet inside a Nairobi data centre hall](/images/atlancis-nairobi-datacentre-hall.webp)

Kenya's demand side is growing fast enough to absorb meaningful imports, but with nuance. DC254 tracks 27 verified facilities in Kenya, of which the operational ones carry 38.5 MW of live designed capacity, against a 230 MW announced pipeline (directory, verified 2026). The largest single campus, iXAfrica's NBOX1 on Mombasa Road, is designed for 22.5 MW and already hosts GPU infrastructure unveiled in November 2025 by Atlancis Technologies, Everse Technology and iXAfrica. A doubling of Ethiopian imports to 400 MW is therefore not an abstract utility manoeuvre; it is incremental energy on the same system that pipeline projects will draw from as they energise between 2026 and 2028.

The caveat is geography and firmness. Nairobi's demand sits at the end of transmission corridors that must also carry hydro dispatch, geothermal baseload and wind variability, and the imported energy competes for the same line capacity. Kenya's own generation build, led by geothermal, continues in parallel precisely because domestic firm capacity is what the grid can dispatch without waiting on a border. The realistic reading is that imports add headroom during dry hydrology years and peak hours, while geothermal remains the anchor for baseload growth, as covered in our geothermal analysis.

## Risks worth pricing in

Four risks deserve a place in any model built on these imports. First, hydrology: Ethiopian exports are firm hydro, and multi-year droughts on the Blue Nile would pressure export commitments just as they pressure domestic supply, the exact scenario Kenya's imports are partly meant to hedge. Second, single-corridor dependence: everything moves on one HVDC line through one converter station, so the loss of Suswa interrupts the trade entirely, and true redundancy only arrives when the next cross-border corridor is built. Third, currency: the import price is foreign-denominated, so the effective shilling cost moves with the exchange rate and feeds through tariffs. Fourth, politics: the Nile dispute has not disappeared, and any future escalation would add a geopolitical premium to an asset Kenyan buyers are being asked to rely on. None of these risks negates the value of the trade; they simply mean it should be modelled as a strong second source of energy, not a replacement for Kenya's own firm generation.

## What to watch

Three markers will show whether the September 2026 announcements harden into delivered megawatts. The first is December 2026, when the doubling to 400 MW is scheduled to begin, and whether actual flows and Kenya Power's procurement follow the announcement. The second is the Eastern Africa Power Pool investment programme, where the reported 1.6 billion US dollar push will either add the second corridor and the regional trading plumbing that makes imports systemic rather than exceptional, or remain a wish list. The third is the Nairobi connection queue: if announced facilities energise on schedule while imports ramp, the market tightens, and the price of firm capacity in Nairobi becomes the number to watch.

## Frequently asked questions

The questions below summarise the points buyers ask about most often regarding the Ethiopia-Kenya power trade.

**How much power does Kenya import from Ethiopia?** Kenya currently imports up to 200 MW over the 500 kV interconnector, with the volume doubling to 400 MW from December 2026 under the announced arrangement.

**Is Ethiopian electricity cheaper than Kenyan power?** The 2022 price was about 6.5 US cents per kWh, and the renewed 2026 arrangement roughly doubles it, which still undercuts Kenyan thermal peaking but no longer looks cheap against Kenya's own geothermal; the value is in system energy and drought hedging.

**Does the interconnector supply data centres directly?** No, it feeds the bulk grid; facilities still buy through normal utility and PPA structures and still engineer their own redundancy behind the meter.

**What happens if the line trips?** Imports stop until the converter station recovers, and Kenya's grid falls back on domestic generation, which is why imports are best modelled as incremental energy rather than firm capacity.
