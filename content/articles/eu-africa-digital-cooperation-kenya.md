Africa's digital infrastructure has become an arena of great-power competition, and Europe has finally published its game plan. A new brief from the European Union Institute for Security Studies — *Africa's digital edge: Building EU-Africa digital cooperation*, by Rossella Marangio and Clotilde Bômont (7 September 2026) — lays out how the EU intends to compete in a market where China builds, America sells, and African governments are, refreshingly, the ones holding the selection committee. For those of us tracking Kenya's data centre market, the document is more than diplomacy: it describes the financing and policy machinery that will shape who builds what, where, over the next decade.

![Policy and regulation shaping Africa's digital infrastructure](/images/dc-policy-regulation.webp)

## The numbers behind the contest

The brief grounds its argument in the market's momentum. Between 2019 and 2024, the share of Africans using the internet rose by 37%. Africa's digital transformation market — valued at **USD 30.24 billion in 2025** — is projected to reach **USD 72.23 billion by 2031**. Those are the stakes the external partners are bidding for. The brief's framing is candid: China, the US, South Korea and India are all actively competing to shape African digital markets, and African governments are responding by pursuing **strategic autonomy, supplier diversification and local value creation** — which is exactly where Europe believes its offer fits.

That offer converges on six priorities: **trusted connectivity, data governance, AI, cybersecurity, digital public infrastructure, and skills**. The word doing the heavy lifting is *trusted* — Europe's differentiation pitch is security, standards and governance-aligned infrastructure, against rivals whose offers are faster, cheaper, or both.

## Kenya and Ghana: the two test cases

The brief's most useful section for our readers is its pairing of country models. **Ghana** illustrates the market-led partnership: a stable, competitive telecoms and cloud environment where European engagement works through commercial frameworks. **Kenya** illustrates the comprehensive model — and here the brief could have been written from our own research notes. Kenya brings East Africa's densest data centre cluster ([26 verified facilities](/directory), the region's deepest interconnection), an existing **EU-Kenya Digital Partnership** with dedicated funding, a regulator with a track record, and a data protection regime that aligns naturally with European standards.

The machinery behind the Kenya relationship is already visible on the ground. The EU's **Global Gateway** framework has channelled significant digital funding toward Kenya — including the EUR 102 million EU-Kenya Digital Partnership package announced in June 2026 and the AfricaConnect research-and-education network boost. The **Digital Investment Facility** — implemented with GIZ and HAUS under the Data Governance in Africa initiative — commissions the Xalam Analytics country data centre briefings we regularly cite ([the free Kenya brief](https://d4dhub.eu/news/dif-country-market-briefs) is the best public market document in this space), alongside green data centre e-learning and cybersecurity guidance. And the **D4D Hub** provides the political umbrella, with Team Europe actively courting African counterparts at events from GITEX Africa to the New Space Africa conference.

## The honest reading: frameworks are cheap, financing is not

The brief is admirably clear-eyed about its own risks, and three of its caveats deserve to be quoted in every Kenyan boardroom that touches European infrastructure money. First, **success depends on financing, implementation and local ownership** — cooperation frameworks are abundant in African digital policy; shovels and switchgear are not. Second, the EU must deliver **credible alternatives** to other external partners, which means matching their speed and price points, not just their values. Third, everything must be **African-led** — the brief explicitly frames the future as a coherent, African-led integration of security, infrastructure, markets and governance, not a European checklist.

For Kenya's data centre sector specifically, the practical translation is this: European money is most likely to arrive as **enablers rather than landlords**. Expect EU-aligned financing for power infrastructure (the binding constraint on every hyperscale announcement, as the Microsoft–G42 stall demonstrated), for skills programmes that feed the [careers pipeline](/careers), and for governance frameworks that make Kenyan-hosted data trustworthy across borders. Do not expect the EU to build data centres directly — that is private capital's job, with the EU de-risking what private capital cannot price.

## What to watch in the Kenyan market

Three indicators will show whether the playbook is working. One: whether Global Gateway and DIF financing visibly moves Kenya's power constraint — new geothermal and transmission capacity is the precondition for every 50 MW-class data centre ambition in the country. Two: whether the EU-Kenya Digital Partnership produces *interoperable* data governance — actual mechanisms that let Kenyan-hosted data serve European customers under adequacy-style arrangements, which would make local colocation dramatically more valuable to multinationals. Three: whether African institutions, not Brussels pressrooms, own the narrative — the brief's own test. Our [regulation coverage](/articles/kenya-data-centre-licensing-framework) and [market-in-numbers reference](/articles/kenya-data-centre-market-numbers) will keep tracking all three, because the party that builds Kenya's digital edge will be decided less in strategy documents than in power purchase agreements, cable landings and the unglamorous work of verification.
