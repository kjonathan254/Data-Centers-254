# Africa has 3% of EMEA’s data-centre capacity. Where does Kenya sit?

**Deck:** A new DC Byte analysis puts Africa at the edge of EMEA’s 93 GW data-centre market. Kenya’s answer is not simply more land or a larger announcement. It is a combination of renewable generation, subsea connectivity, a growing interconnection layer and a pipeline that must still convert into operational capacity.

**Category:** Market intelligence  
**Reading time:** 8 minutes  
**Updated:** October 2026

A new market analysis has put Africa’s data-centre opportunity into one uncomfortable number: **3%**.

DC Byte’s latest EMEA analysis estimates the region’s total data-centre capacity at **93 GW**, spanning live facilities, construction, committed projects and early-stage developments. Europe accounts for **88%** of that total. The Middle East accounts for **9%**. Africa accounts for just **3%**. [1]

That is a small share of a large market. It is also the reason the continent is attracting attention.

The question for Kenya is not whether Africa has room to grow. It clearly does. The more useful question is whether Kenya can turn its advantages—renewable generation, subsea cable access, established interconnection and a growing digital economy—into capacity that is actually powered, connected and operational.

## The 3% is an opportunity, not a forecast

The first point needs to be stated precisely. The 3% figure is **Africa’s share of EMEA data-centre capacity**, not Africa’s share of global capacity. It is also a regional comparison across a broad DC Byte dataset that includes operational capacity and multiple stages of development. It should not be read as a forecast that Africa will automatically capture the next 97% of growth.

The number does, however, show how unevenly digital infrastructure is distributed.

Europe’s established hubs still dominate. Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin—often grouped as FLAP-D—account for almost a quarter of EMEA’s total capacity. But those markets are facing the constraints that increasingly determine where new capacity can be delivered: available land, grid access, planning timelines and the ability to serve large power-intensive campuses. [1]

DC Byte’s analysis identifies South Africa as the African market most clearly positioned for the next wave. Land availability is one reason. Power delivery remains the condition. The analysis makes the point that a site is not attractive simply because land is available. Operators still need enough power, delivered within the customer’s timeline, and enough supporting infrastructure for later phases. [1]

That distinction matters for every African market, including Kenya.

## Kenya’s counter-pitch is not one number

Kenya’s current proposition rests on several assets that work together.

The country has a verified data-centre directory of **27 Kenyan facilities**, of which **20 are operational**. DC254’s latest market review separates **10.5 MW of published live IT load** from **42.9 MW of built capacity at operational sites**. Behind that operating base sits a tracked pipeline of **230 MW** across under-construction, committed and early-stage projects. [2]

Those numbers must remain separated.

The 230 MW is not operating capacity. It combines **77 MW under construction**, **53 MW committed** and **100 MW at an early stage**. The distinction is important because projects at different stages face different tests. Under-construction projects are physically underway. Committed projects have land and funding signals but are not yet built. Early-stage projects may still require approvals, financing, firm tenants or power-delivery commitments.

This is exactly the discipline African markets need as the global data-centre conversation shifts toward AI and hyperscale demand. A pipeline is evidence of intent. It is not evidence that the grid, building, cooling plant and network are already serving customers.

## Kenya’s power advantage is real—but it has a ceiling

Kenya’s strongest counter-pitch is the quality of its generation mix. Approximately **93% of Kenya’s electricity generation in 2024 came from renewable sources**, including geothermal, hydro and wind. Geothermal supplies roughly 45% of generation and gives Kenya something that solar-only propositions cannot provide without storage: renewable baseload power. [3]

That is strategically important as data-centre demand becomes more power-intensive. AI workloads are changing the shape of the market. The next facility is not always a conventional colocation building with incremental demand. It may be a large campus that needs substantial power at one location, along with a credible path to expand.

Kenya can therefore make a compelling sustainability argument. A workload hosted on a grid with a high renewable generation share has a different carbon story from one hosted in a coal-heavy system that must assemble its clean-power position through additional contracts and certificates.

But renewable generation is not the same thing as firm, site-specific data-centre power.

Kenya’s grid can have enough capacity in aggregate while a particular site still faces connection, transmission, substation or delivery constraints. DC254’s market review identifies the stalled Microsoft–G42 project as the clearest reminder: delivering 100 MW or more to one site is a different engineering and commercial problem from serving a growing city. [2]

That is why the strongest Kenya pitch is not “we have green power.” It is:

> **Kenya has a high-renewable generation base, but now needs to prove that clean power can be delivered reliably, at scale and on the timelines that large digital-infrastructure customers require.**

## Connectivity gives Kenya a second advantage

Power is only one part of the location decision. Kenya also has a credible connectivity foundation.

Seven live subsea cable systems connect to the Kenyan coast. Africa-1 is landed but remains pending a confirmed ready-for-service date. Daraja is announced, and LuLu is planned. The cable tracker separates those categories rather than counting every announced route as live. [4]

That coastline matters because Kenya’s data-centre ecosystem is split across two related roles.

**Mombasa is the international gateway.** It is where cable systems land and where international routes enter the country.

**Nairobi is the infrastructure cluster.** It concentrates data centres, enterprise demand, operators, networks and interconnection.

The national opportunity lies in the connection between the two: subsea cables, landing stations, terrestrial fibre, Nairobi facilities, internet exchanges and the businesses that consume digital services.

The interconnection data supports that argument. In DC254’s latest PeeringDB cross-check, Africa Data Centres Nairobi 1 leads the listed facilities with **122 networks and four internet exchanges on site**. iColo Mombasa One follows with **94 networks and three exchanges**, while iColo Nairobi One has **62 networks and four exchanges**. These are public registration signals, not a complete measure of facility quality, but they show where connectivity depth is already accumulating. [2]

Kenya’s pitch is therefore broader than available land. It is a connected market with a coastal gateway, a large urban demand centre and an interconnection layer that is becoming more visible.

## The real competition is deliverability

Africa does not need more generic claims about being the next data-centre frontier. It needs to demonstrate delivery.

DC Byte’s analysis identifies the right test: power availability and the time required to deliver it. It also warns that early-stage pipelines can exaggerate the amount of capacity likely to become operational. Milan, for example, is cited as a market where a large share of total capacity remains at an early development stage. Announcements should not automatically be treated as completed facilities. [1]

Kenya’s own figures make the same argument.

The country has **230 MW of tracked announced pipeline**, but only **10.5 MW of published live IT load** and **42.9 MW of built operational capacity**. That is not a weakness in the data. It is the point of staging the data honestly.

For Kenya to move from a credible emerging market to a preferred destination for the next wave, four conversions matter:

1. **Pipeline to operational capacity:** projects must be completed and commissioned.
2. **Generation to firm delivery:** renewable electricity must reach large sites with the required reliability and redundancy.
3. **Connectivity to customer value:** cable access must translate into networks, cloud on-ramps, IXPs and low-latency services.
4. **Announcements to evidence:** every major claim must acquire a source, a status and a verification date.

## Where Kenya sits in the African race

Kenya is not South Africa. It does not have the same depth of installed capacity, operator footprint or established hyperscale market.

It should not compete by pretending otherwise.

Kenya’s stronger position is as a market with a distinctive combination of assets:

- A renewable-heavy electricity-generation mix
- Seven live subsea cable systems
- A growing Nairobi interconnection cluster
- A mature mobile, fintech and enterprise demand base
- 27 tracked facilities, with 20 operational
- A 230 MW pipeline that is large enough to matter but still needs to be converted
- A location from which operators can serve East Africa and connect to wider regional markets

DC Byte’s earlier Africa market work identified South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt and Morocco as the continent’s main data-centre hubs. [5] The latest EMEA analysis adds a sharper question: which markets can deliver the next wave when established hubs are constrained?

Kenya’s answer should not be a slogan about becoming the next Johannesburg. It should be an evidence-backed proposition:

> **Kenya can offer clean generation, international connectivity and a growing digital market—but its next competitive advantage will be the ability to deliver reliable power and commissioned capacity faster than its pipeline suggests.**

## The question for 2027

The next phase of Kenya’s market will be decided less by the size of the announcement pipeline than by what becomes operational.

DC254’s current watchlist includes the completion of iXAfrica NBOX1.2, Africa Data Centres Nairobi 2, Nxtra by Airtel’s Tatu City project and iXAfrica NBOX2 at Tilisi. It also includes the decision path around the Microsoft–G42 AI project and the interconnection ramp of Digital Realty’s NBO2. [2]

These projects will show whether Kenya can move from a market with strong ingredients to a market with repeatable delivery.

Africa’s 3% share of EMEA capacity is not a verdict. It is a starting position. The continent has room, demand and increasingly visible connectivity. But the next wave will go to markets that can answer the operational questions:

- Is the power available?
- Can it be delivered to the site?
- How quickly can the facility be built?
- Is the route diversity real?
- Are the networks and customers already present?
- What portion of the pipeline is genuinely committed?

Kenya has a credible answer to several of those questions already. The country’s opportunity is to make the remaining answers measurable.

That is where the race begins.
